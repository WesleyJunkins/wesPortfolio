Personal Statement

The world of technology was never something that piqued my interest; the concept of how a computer processed 1's and 0's seemed far removed from the career I envisioned for myself as a psychiatrist. Little did I know that these two worlds would interconnect to produce a lifelong passion. Growing up in rural Alabama, I rarely encountered technology and felt more intimidated than inspired when we finally used them in the classroom. In one comical case, we were told to shut down our classroom computers, and I believed that by powering off the monitor, I was shutting down my machine. This humorous incident exemplifies my encounters with technology before I received my own personal computer.

One quiet afternoon, my older brother and I unearthed an old Hewlett-Packard Pavilion 7855 from my father's woodshop. As the behemoth machine booted into the Windows Millenium Edition operating system, my thirteen-year-old brain was flooded with questions about the most rudimentary computing paradigms: "How does it paint a window on the screen?" "How does the mouse track movement and adjust the cursor on the screen so quickly?" This curiosity soon shifted from hardware to the logic of software, and I began teaching myself how code could manipulate a machine's behavior. With no computer science courses available at school, I turned to online resources and libraries, immersing myself in operating systems, software, and programming. From that point forward, computer science became the driving force behind my curiosity and ambition.

Upon graduating from high school, I enrolled at Shelton State Community College, where I was inducted into the Phi Theta Kappa honor society as a research officer. I led a research project on community college students' mental health, inspired by my interest in psychiatry. This project earned a regional award and introduced me to academic research. Later, in a summer research program at the University of Alabama's Chemical Engineering department, I helped develop environmentally friendly solvents to dissolve plastic waste and presented our findings in Washington, D.C. These experiences ignited my zeal for rigorous academic inquiry and my desire to embark on a lifelong career in academia.

After graduating from Shelton State, I was blessed to receive a full-ride scholarship to the University of Alabama to study computer science. Eager to join a research lab, I reached out to several professors but was turned away, as I had not yet narrowed down my interests in computer science. After reflecting on my fascination with psychiatry and the brain, I realized I wanted to connect psychology with computing. Exploring this further, I discovered the field of human-computer interaction (HCI), which aligned perfectly with my passions.

Around this time, I discovered a flyer for the Brain Drone Race (BDR) and was astonished that a drone could be controlled by only my thoughts. After joining the BDR development team, I researched the lab that conducted this project: the Human-Technology Interaction Lab (HTIL), where I learned they specialized in HCI and Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI). Later that semester, Dr. Crawford, who leads the HTIL, mentioned in a keynote address that his lab needed undergraduates with JavaScript skills. Although I had never used JavaScript, I immediately emailed him, expressing both my interest and willingness to learn new skills. To my surprise, I was accepted into the HTIL and assigned programming projects that expanded my skills.

Over time, Dr. Crawford guided me to become a more capable scientist. During my time in the lab, I assisted with multiple research projects by developing systems and helping conduct literature reviews. Graduate students mentored me in navigating the research process, from refining literature reviews to writing my first IRB protocol and designing a study. In addition to these experiences, I also had the privilege of attending my first computer science conference, the International Conference on Robotics and Automation (ICRA), in 2025.

In the HTIL, I gained hands-on experience with a range of tools and frameworks for extended reality (XR), including Babylon.js, Apple's Create ML, and WebXR. Using these alongside MQTT, ROS, and low-level socket programming, I built XR systems that integrated seamlessly with robotics applications, most notably the extended reality brain drone race.

I also developed a drone simulation in Webots using Python's Pytorch library and socket programming, where a neuroevolutionary algorithm adapts flight based on brain and muscle signals while following a circular path. In collaboration with a professor from another university, I helped design an olfactory computing system using a modified mBot and custom Python GUI, later extending it with an XR pond simulation that controlled scent release through a microcontroller. These projects collectively deepened my appreciation for academic research and reinforced my commitment to pursuing this career path.

I am eager for the opportunity to continue my research and contribute to the academic community through HCI and BCI research, specifically in applications involving health HCI and human-centered neurotechnology for healthcare. My background in XR systems, machine learning, and interdisciplinary collaborations has prepared me to bring both technical expertise and research capabilities to advancing this field. Pursuing a Ph.D. will not only sharpen my skills as a scientist but also provide the foundation for a career dedicated to academic research and the development of technologies that meaningfully enhance human experiences.

Projects

Brain Drone Race

The Brain Drone Race (BDR) is a novel human-computer interaction (HCI) application that leverages brain activity to control a drone. The BDR is a multi-party application that allows multiple users to compete against each other in a race. The users wear an EEG headset and use their brain activity to control the drone. In the past, we have used users' emotions, focus, and motor imagery to control the drone. When the EEG signals are captured and preprocessed, we use a machine learning classifier to convert the signals to drone instructions.

hciXR

hciXR is a proposed JavaScript library that allows developers to create extended reality (XR) and 3D applications with human-computer interaction (HCI) features. It is in the planning phase currently and will soon transition to ongoing development. The library will be open-source and will be available on npm. The goal of the project is to remove the complicated process of designing 3D interfaces and BCI (Brain-Computer Interface) and MCI (Muscle-Computer Interface) functionality from the developer's workflow so they can focus on the application logic.

Neuroevolutionary Algorithms and Computer Vision for Circular Lap-Based Physiological Drone Racing

Robots, including drones, have been able to follow lines in flight for a long time. However, following a circular path is a more challenging task. In this project, we are using neuroevolution and computer vision to train a drone to follow a circular path. We are using a drone with a camera and a neuroevolutionary algorithm-trained agent to learn to follow a circular path. The goal of this project is to explore the use of neuroevolutionary algorithms and computer vision to train a drone to follow a circular path in the hope of transitioning the brain drone race to a lap-based race.

Olfactory Human-Computer Interaction

This project explores the use of olfactory senses in human-computer interaction. We built a system that allows the user to view an extended reality environment containing a Koi Fish pond. The user can lean towards or away from the pond. Behind the scenes, distance measurements control the amount of scent the user receives. The aim of this study is to measure physiological responses to smell when trying to complete a task (e.g. counting the Koi Fish).

Quadruped Robotics and Extended Reality in Search and Rescue Tasks

In this project, we aim to develop a complex system of communication between a quadruped robot and an extended reality device. The goal of this project is to assist in search and rescue operations where the user may not be able to traverse environment that a quadruped can. The interaction should ideally be seamless and require low cognitive workload.

Quadruped Robotics vs. Wheeled Mobile Robotics in K-12 STEM Education

This project presents a user study comparing the use of a quadruped robot and a wheeled mobile robot in a K-12 STEM classroom. The goal of this project is to measure self-efficacy, collaboration, and engagement from students studying the same curriculum, but with different robots (quadruped vs. wheeled).

Extended Reality Brain Drone Racing

The Brain-Drone Race (BDR) is a novel human-computer interaction (HCI) application that uses a participants' EEG signals to control a real drone. This project aims to recreate the experience inside an extended reality environment. The goal of this project is to explore the differences in user engagement and performance using the two systems.

PhysioLab

PhysioLab is a proposed application that facilitates the development of physiological applications. The program will include built-in data collection programs for multiple physiological devices. It also introduces the Physiological Scripting Language (PSL), which helps developers with streaming data in, filtering it, and streaming it out to other programs, such as machine learning applications or physiological games.

Machine Learning Object Detection and Manipulation in Extended Reality

This project explores the use of an Apple Vision Pro to detect specific objects in real-time and outline them. The program uses machine learning to learn the dimensions of any object. Then, in the extended reality scene, if the object is detected, a grid outline of the object will appear and follow the object during translations and rotations.

Smart Controller App

For my computer science capstone project, my team and I developed a mobile app that allows the user to control the operations of a home-heating stove remotely. The app was developed using Flutter and was available on both iOS and Android. The app connected to a Raspberry Pi over the internet via MQTT. I built a program on the Raspberry Pi that converted MQTT messages to bluetooth messages and sent them to the stove's controller. Additionally, the Pi connected to a Firebase database, which was capable of storing data from the stove over time.

Publications

"It Wasn't As Bad As I Thought": Exploring K-12 Students' Experiences with Real-Time and Pre-Recorded Physiological Data

Status: Accepted

Physiological sensing technologies are becoming increasingly accessible, prompting educators and researchers to explore hands-on approaches for introducing K–12 students to physiological computing. This paper presents findings from a study in which students engaged in two sets of activities: one using a consumer-grade hardware physiological sensor (i.e., OpenBCI Ganglion) and the other using pre-recorded physiological data. Our observations revealed similar outcomes and student engagement across both approaches. Notably, students reported increases in self-efficacy and confidence regardless of whether they worked with real-time or pre-recorded data.

PhysioBots: Engaging K-12 Students with Physiological Computing and Robotics

April, 2025

To address the limited empirical work evaluating ways to expose K-12 students to physiological computing, we present PhysioBots, an educational tool designed to introduce K-12 students to physiological computing and robotics. We evaluated PhysioBots with 27 high school students to compare physiological (e.g., self-induced changes in brain or muscle activity) and conventional control (e.g., keyboard) of a robot during a STEM education activity. Our preliminary results suggest that PhysioBots may improve students' self-efficacy and programming confidence.

PhysioML: A Web-Based Tool for Machine Learning Education with Real-Time Physiological Data

February, 2025

This paper presents findings from a study exploring students' experiences learning basic machine learning concepts while using physiological sensors to control an interactive game. Activities featuring physiological sensors produced similar outcomes when compared to exercises that leveraged image data. While students' machine learning self-efficacy increased in both conditions, students seemed more curious about machine learning after working with the physiological sensor.
