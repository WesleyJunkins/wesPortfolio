Wesley Junkins

Human-AI Interaction · Physiological Computing & BCI · Trust & Evaluation of AI Systems

I'm a PhD student in human-centered computing at the University of Florida, working with Dr. Juan E. Gilbert in the Computing for Social Good Lab.

About

My research is moving toward the intersection of human-AI interaction and physiological computing: how signals from the body and the world are interpreted by AI systems, and how we evaluate whether the result serves the people it's meant to serve.

I got here through physiological computing and computing education. At the University of Alabama's Human-Technology Interaction Lab, as an undergraduate and then a master's student, I worked on the multi-party BCI architecture behind the Brain-Drone Race, co-authored studies teaching K-12 students with their own brain and muscle signals, and ran user studies of brain-controlled drone racing across XR, desktop, and real-drone conditions. My thesis used neuroevolution and imitation learning to teach a drone to follow a path on its own.

That work taught me two things: a physiological signal is only useful once a system interprets it well, and the hard question is whether people can trust and use the result. My dissertation work at UF will carry those questions into human-AI interaction: physiological sensing as the foundation, evaluation of AI systems as the focus, alongside the lab's research on trustworthy voting technology.

I'm a first-generation college student from Gordo, Alabama, and a McKnight Doctoral Fellow.

Where they meet

My work sits in human-centered computing and human-computer interaction (HCI), at the intersection of physiological computing and human-AI interaction: how signals from the body and the world are interpreted by AI systems, and how we evaluate whether the result serves the people it's meant to serve.

Sensing

Sensing

Physiological and multimodal signals: EEG and muscle activity today; wearables and biometric sensing more broadly. The raw material, what the body and the world are saying before any system interprets it.

Meaning
Evaluation

Meaning and Evaluation

Meaning. The AI and machine-learning layer that turns those signals into something a system can act on.

Evaluation. Whether the result is trustworthy, usable, and good for the person, measured with real participants.

Sensing → Meaning → Evaluation

Human-AI Interaction · Physiological Computing & BCI · Trust & Evaluation of AI Systems

Sensing becomes meaning, and meaning has to be evaluated. My research direction is that whole chain: physiological and multimodal sensing as the foundation, AI that interprets those signals, and human-centered evaluation of whether the result is trustworthy, usable, and good for the person.

Application areas I care about: clinical and neuropsychiatric health, accessibility, and trustworthy civic technology.

BDR-XR: Brain-Drone Racing in Extended Reality

Active · 2026

Does the medium change the experience of a brain-computer interface? I designed and ran a within-subjects study (n = 13) in which participants raced a drone with a Muse EEG headset across three counterbalanced conditions: Apple Vision Pro, a 3D scene on a monitor, and a real drone. I measured cognitive load (NASA-TLX), intrinsic motivation (IMI), and usability (SUS). I wrote the IRB protocol, built the study software, and led data collection. Analysis is underway for publication.

Physiological Computing for K-12 Learning

Published · 2025 – 2026 · PhysioBots · PhysioML

Tools and studies that introduce students to physiological computing and machine learning using their own brain and muscle signals. PhysioBots was evaluated with 27 high-school students, comparing physiological and keyboard control of a robot; a follow-up study compared real-time and pre-recorded physiological data. Across studies, students reported gains in self-efficacy and programming confidence. I co-authored three papers from this line of work.

"It Wasn't As Bad As I Thought": Exploring K-12 Students' Experiences with Real-Time and Pre-Recorded Physiological Data

Published · SIGCSE TS 2026 · Proceedings of the 57th ACM Technical Symposium on Computer Science Education V. 1

Students completed two sets of activities, one with a consumer-grade physiological sensor (OpenBCI Ganglion) and one with pre-recorded physiological data. Outcomes and engagement were similar across both, and students reported increases in self-efficacy and confidence either way.

PhysioBots: Engaging K-12 Students with Physiological Computing and Robotics

Published · CHI EA '25 · April 2025

Myles Lewis, Pranay Joshi, Vincent Ingram, Wesley Junkins, Chris S. Crawford.

PhysioBots introduces K-12 students to physiological computing and robotics. Evaluated with 27 high-school students comparing physiological and keyboard control of a robot, it showed gains in students' self-efficacy and programming confidence.

PhysioML: A Web-Based Tool for Machine Learning Education with Real-Time Physiological Data

Published · SIGCSE TS 2025 · February 2025

Bryan Y. Hernández-Cuevas, Myles Lewis, Wesley Junkins, Chris S. Crawford, Andre Denham, Feiya Luo.

Students learned basic machine-learning concepts while using physiological sensors to control a game. Machine-learning self-efficacy rose in both the sensor and image-data conditions, and students seemed more curious about machine learning after working with the physiological sensor.
